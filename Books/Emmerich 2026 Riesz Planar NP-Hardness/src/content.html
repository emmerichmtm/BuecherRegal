Riesz \(s\)-Energy Subset Selection is NP‑Hard in the Euclidean Plane

A rational reduction from planar cubic Independent Set
via diagonal selectors at exponent \(s=2\)
❧
Michael Emmerich¹ · Ksenia Pereverdieva² · Andre Deutz²
¹University of Jyväskylä, Finland  ·  ²Leiden University, The Netherlands
August 2026

Abstract

We prove that minimum Riesz \(s\)-energy subset selection in the Euclidean plane is NP-complete already for the fixed exponent \(s=2\). The reduction uses Barahona’s planar cubic Ising model with uniform field. A spin is encoded by one diagonal of a four-point square: axis-aligned selector chains implement ferromagnetic consistency, while a \(45^\circ\) terminal geometry yields an antiferromagnetic source interaction. Rational diagonal perturbations realize the magnetic field, and all remaining interactions are dominated by polynomial separation. Because \(s=2\) and all coordinates are rational, every constructed energy and the decision threshold are rational exactly.

Contents

Introduction

For a finite set \(P\subset\R^d\), an integer \(k\), and \(s>0\), define the Riesz \(s\)-energy of \(S\subseteq P\) by

\[E_s(S)=\sum_{\{p,q\}\subset S}\frac1{\norm{p-q}^s}.\]

The minimum Riesz-energy subset problem asks for a \(k\)-point subset of minimum energy. General-metric hardness is known [5]; Euclidean hardness in the plane is known when the exponent is part of the input [6]. The purpose of this paper is to fix both geometric parameters at

\[d=2,\qquad s=2.\]

The planar difficulty is representational rather than topological. A lifted binary pair, which is convenient in three dimensions, has no common normal direction in the plane. We instead use a four-point diagonal selector: four candidate points form a very small square and the two logical states are its two diagonals.

The selector has an angular multipole law. Axis-aligned neighboring selectors induce a field-free ferromagnetic coupling, whereas a center vector at angle \(45^\circ\) induces an antiferromagnetic coupling. This allows the same local object to implement consistency wires and source interactions. At exponent two the spin–spin term decays as \(r^{-6}\), which remains summable with enough slack at bends and degree-three junctions.

The source problem is the planar Ising model with magnetic field appearing in Barahona’s classical hardness proof [1]. We start from the decision version of Independent Set restricted to planar cubic graphs, which is NP-complete; Uehara proves the stronger statement that NP-completeness persists for 3-connected cubic planar graphs of girth greater than three [2], and Mohar independently proves NP-hardness of minimum vertex cover for 2-connected cubic planar graphs [3]. Barahona’s problem P4 is maximum stable set on a planar cubic graph, and his reduction to P5 keeps the same interaction graph while introducing uniform antiferromagnetic couplings and magnetic field \(1\). Thus P5 is already NP-hard on planar cubic interaction graphs. For completeness, we reproduce the reduction algebraically below, so the proof does not rely on P5 as a black-box hardness theorem; the resulting fixed gap is central to the threshold argument.

A useful simplification is specific to \(s=2\). For rational points \(p,q\in\Q^2\),

\[\norm{p-q}^{-2}=\frac1{(p_1-q_1)^2+(p_2-q_2)^2}\in\Q.\]

Hence the field compensation and the final threshold can be performed with exact rational arithmetic. This also gives membership in NP.

The proof architecture is

\[\boxed{\begin{array}{c}\text{two-of-four planar selector}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{axis/diagonal angular coupling law}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{exact rational first-order field compensation}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{planar orthogonal consistency trees}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{one positive objective box per source edge}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{Barahona Hamiltonian plus a small remainder}.\end{array}}\]
Theorem 1.1  Main theorem

The decision problem \(\RSSP(2,2)\) is NP-complete. More precisely, from an instance \((G,K)\) of maximum independent set on a planar cubic graph one can construct in polynomial time a finite rational point set \(P\subset\Q^2\), an integer \(k\), and a rational threshold \(\tau\) such that

\[\exists S\subseteq P,\quad |S|=k,\quad E_2(S)\le\tau\]

if and only if \(G\) has an independent set of cardinality at least \(K\).

Proof organization. Section 2 gives a formula-light walkthrough of the reduction and the hierarchy of energy scales. Section 3 then states the planar cubic Ising source precisely. Sections 4–6 establish the selector, coupling, and field-compensation lemmas. Sections 7–9 build and normalize the planar consistency trees. Sections 10–12 identify the exact source Hamiltonian, choose the rational threshold, and prove polynomial encoding. Appendix E records the main adversarial checks.

How the reduction works: a high-level walkthrough

This section gives the mental model for the construction before the quantitative estimates begin. The reduction has two conceptual layers. First, a maximum independent set instance on a planar cubic graph is written as a planar Ising ground-state problem. Second, that Ising system is realized by a set of rational points in the plane whose minimum Riesz \(2\)-energy subset behaves like the Ising ground state. The technical sections that follow are devoted to proving that the geometric realization is stable against all unwanted long-range interactions.

From a cubic graph to an Ising system

Let \(G\) be a planar cubic graph. We attach one binary spin to every vertex. We interpret one spin state as “selected” and the other as “not selected.” The Ising Hamiltonian used by Barahona contains two ingredients: an antiferromagnetic interaction on every graph edge and the same one-spin field at every vertex.

It is useful to be precise about the role of the field. With the sign convention used in this paper, the field term by itself does not literally reward the selected state. Rather, because every source vertex has degree three, the three incident edge terms and the one local field term combine in a particularly simple way. If \(t\) vertices are declared selected and \(q\) graph edges have both endpoints selected, then, up to a fixed additive constant, the Ising objective is

\[4(q-t).\]

Thus adding a selected vertex gives a benefit of four units, while every edge whose two endpoints are both selected costs four units. Any selected set containing an internal edge can therefore be repaired without losing objective value, and the ground state encodes a maximum independent set. Section 3 gives the exact identity and the resulting gap.

One selector box is one binary variable

A logical spin is represented by four candidate points at the corners of a very small square. The global cardinality is chosen so that, after the forcing argument, exactly two points are selected from every square. Adjacent corners are closer than opposite corners, so a side pair has noticeably larger Riesz energy than a diagonal pair. Consequently the only low-energy two-point choices are the two diagonals.

We designate one diagonal as spin \(+1\) and the other as spin \(-1\). In this sense a selector box is a geometric binary variable. The selector-forcing penalty is intentionally enormous compared with every later energy scale: it is of order \(b^{-2}\), while the logical spin interactions are of order \(b^4\). This first scale separation ensures that the optimization problem cannot profit by using the wrong number of points in a box or by choosing a side instead of a diagonal.

How a cubic vertex becomes a selector tree

A cubic source vertex has exactly three incident edges. After taking a crossing-free orthogonal drawing of the planar graph, we replace one source vertex by one rooted selector tree \(T_v\). Topologically, this tree is extremely simple: it is a three-leaf star. There is one root selector and three internally disjoint rectilinear branches, one for each incident graph edge. The branches may be long and may contain right-angle bends, but there is no recursive branching and no exponential gadget growth.

Along each branch, selector centers are placed at unit spacing on horizontal or vertical segments. For such axis-aligned neighboring selectors the effective Riesz coupling is ferromagnetic: equal selector states have lower energy than unequal states. The chain therefore acts as a wire carrying one binary value through the planar drawing. Bends and the degree-three junction at the root are handled by the same consistency analysis.

Only one designated selector, the root of \(T_v\), is assigned the nonzero target one-spin field. The dummy selectors along the wires have target field zero. Small rational perturbations of the selector diagonals cancel the unwanted geometric one-spin fields and leave precisely this intended pattern. Thus the field is a local part of the Ising encoding; it is not implemented by a separate long-range gadget.

How a graph edge becomes a \(45^\circ\) objective box

For every source edge \(uv\), one branch of \(T_u\) and one branch of \(T_v\) terminate in a small objective box. Their terminal prefixes are arranged so that the unique closest cross-tree pair of selector centers is separated by a \(45^\circ\) vector. At this angle the sign of the effective selector coupling is opposite to the axis-aligned wire coupling: it is antiferromagnetic.

The distinguished \(45^\circ\) pair is accompanied by a halo of more distant cross-prefix interactions. We do not pretend that those interactions vanish. Instead we bound their complete absolute contribution and prove that the positive distinguished interaction dominates the halo. Every source edge therefore realizes the same positive objective coefficient \(\lambda\). After the consistency trees have been normalized, that box contributes exactly the intended Ising interaction between the logical spins of \(u\) and \(v\), up to the globally controlled perturbation terms.

The hierarchy of penalties

The construction is organized around a strict hierarchy of energy scales. It is helpful to keep the following four levels in mind.

  • Level 1: Selector legality. The very large intra-box penalty forces exactly one diagonal to be chosen in each four-point selector. This scale is of order \(b^{-2}\) and dominates everything else.
  • Level 2: Tree consistency. Once every box is a valid spin, the strongest logical interaction is the nearest-neighbor axis coupling along the selector trees. A disagreeing wire edge can be repaired by flipping the entire component on the leaf side of that edge. The gain from repairing the cut edge is larger than the sum of all adverse same-tree tails, one possible objective-box load, and all residual and remote terms. Hence every energy minimum has a monochromatic tree \(T_v\).
  • Level 3: The intended source Hamiltonian. After normalization, each whole tree represents a single spin. The objective box on every source edge and the root field at every source vertex are deliberately matched to the same coefficient \(\lambda\). These are not separate asymptotic scales: together they reproduce Barahona’s Ising Hamiltonian.
  • Level 4: Remainder terms. All unintended long-range interactions, residual fields, and coupling changes caused by the small rational perturbations are forced below a small fraction of \(\lambda\). They are therefore too weak to change the yes/no decision inherited from the fixed source gap.

This hierarchy separates two logically different tasks. Levels 1 and 2 prove normalization: arbitrary Riesz subsets are pushed into the intended discrete state space. Levels 3 and 4 prove simulation: once normalized, the remaining energy is the desired Ising energy plus a harmless error.

Why long wires do not destroy the reduction

Riesz energy is long-range: every selected point interacts with every other selected point. Hence a long selector tree cannot simply be treated as a nearest-neighbor spin chain. The proof controls this in two different ways.

First consider interactions inside one tree. If a defective consistency edge is cut and the component away from the root is flipped, point pairs across that cut occur at many separations. At spin level the unwanted coupling decays like the sixth power of the distance. Along a one-dimensional wire, the number of cross-cut pairs at separation \(k\) grows only linearly in \(k\), so the complete tail is bounded by a convergent sum of the form \(\sum k^{-5}\). Right-angle bends and the degree-three junction lead to similarly convergent lattice sums. Crucially, these bounds are uniform: they do not grow with the total length of the branch.

Second, geometrically unrelated features are placed far apart. The orthogonal drawing is blown up by a polynomial separation scale \(D\). Even if we pessimistically count all \(M^2\) possible remote selector pairs, each remote spin–spin coupling is at most a constant times \(b^4D^{-6}\). The chosen polynomial scale makes the complete remote load tiny compared with one intended objective coefficient. Thus increasing the wire length only increases \(M\) polynomially; the separation is chosen large enough that the total far field still decreases to a negligible level.

The three-leaf topology gives one further simplification in the component-flip proof. Deleting a consistency edge and taking the component away from the root can expose at most one source-edge objective box. Therefore a repair never has to pay for several objective interactions at once.

Collapsing a tree to one bit

The word “collapse” refers to a proof step, not to a geometric operation. Before normalization, a tree containing many selectors has many possible spin assignments. The component-flip lemma proves that every assignment containing a consistency defect can be strictly improved. Hence only two relevant states remain: all selectors in \(T_v\) are \(+1\), or all are \(-1\).

At that point we replace the entire tree in the analysis by one logical variable \(\sigma_v\). Every same-tree spin product is then constant and can be absorbed into a baseline energy. What remains is one root field for each source vertex, one objective interaction for each source edge, and a small remainder. In other words, the large geometric instance reduces to the original Ising system at the level of its normalized states.

The final correctness argument therefore has the following conceptual form:

\[\boxed{\begin{array}{c}\text{planar cubic Independent Set}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{planar Ising ground state}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{normalized selector trees}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{Riesz }2\text{-energy subset selection}\end{array}}\]

The remainder is made much smaller than the fixed gap between consecutive independent-set values, so it cannot change which side of the decision threshold the instance lies on.

The planar cubic source and its gap

Let \(G=(V,E)\) be planar and cubic, with \(n=|V|\) and \(m=|E|=3n/2\). The threshold decision problem

\[\text{“does \(G\) contain an independent set of size at least \(K\)?”}\]

is NP-complete on this graph class [2]; indeed the cited result holds under the additional assumptions of 3-connectivity and girth greater than three. Equivalently, minimum vertex cover is NP-hard even on 2-connected cubic planar graphs [3], using \(\alpha(G)+\operatorname{vc}(G)=|V|\).

Barahona formulates the source optimization problem as P4, maximum stable set in a planar cubic graph, and then defines P5 on a planar graph with all interactions antiferromagnetic and uniform magnetic field \(1\) [1]. In Barahona’s reduction from P4 to P5 the Ising instance uses the same graph \(G\). Consequently P5 remains NP-hard even when its interaction graph is restricted to be planar and cubic. Rather than appeal to that statement as a black box, we now write the many-one reduction to the threshold form explicitly. We use the equivalent Hamiltonian

\[H_B(\sigma)=\sum_{uv\in E}\sigma_u\sigma_v+\sum_{v\in V}\sigma_v,\qquad \sigma_v\in\sgnspin.\tag{1}\]
Proposition 3.1  Explicit source reduction

Given a planar cubic Independent Set instance \((G,K)\), let

\[H_0=\frac{|V|}{2}-4K.\]

Then \(G\) has an independent set of size at least \(K\) if and only if there exists a spin assignment \(\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{V}\) with \(H_B(\sigma)\le H_0\). Membership in NP is immediate because \(H_B(\sigma)\) is an integer sum that can be evaluated in polynomial time. Hence this restricted Ising threshold problem is NP-complete, and its optimization version is NP-hard.

For completeness, set

\[x_v=\frac{1+\sigma_v}{2}\in\{0,1\},\qquad t=\sum_v x_v,\]

and let \(q\) be the number of edges with both endpoints in \(\{v:x_v=1\}\). Since \(G\) is cubic,

\[\sum_{uv\in E}(x_u+x_v)=3t.\]

Substitution into (1) gives

\[H_B(\sigma)=\frac n2+4(q-t).\tag{2}\]

Every \(t\)-vertex set inducing \(q\) edges contains an independent set of size at least \(t-q\): choose one endpoint from every induced edge and delete the union of those at most \(q\) chosen vertices. Conversely an independent set of size \(\alpha(G)\) has \(q=0\) and \(t=\alpha(G)\). Hence

\[\min_\sigma H_B(\sigma)=\frac n2-4\alpha(G).\tag{3}\]

In particular, for an independent-set target \(K\),

\[\begin{aligned}\alpha(G)\ge K&\Longrightarrow \min H_B\le H_0,&\quad&(4)\\ \alpha(G)\le K-1&\Longrightarrow \min H_B\ge H_0+4,&&(5)\end{aligned}\]

where

\[H_0=\frac n2-4K.\tag{6}\]

The fixed gap \(4\) will absorb all geometric approximation errors.

Diagonal selector cells

The unperturbed selector

For a center \(c\in\R^2\) and scale \(b>0\), define the four candidate points

\[c+(b,b),\quad c+(b,-b),\quad c+(-b,b),\quad c+(-b,-b).\]

The two intended states are the diagonals

\[\begin{aligned}S_+(c)&=\{c+(b,b),c-(b,b)\},\\ S_-(c)&=\{c+(b,-b),c-(b,-b)\}.\end{aligned}\]

We identify them with spins \(\sigma=+1\) and \(\sigma=-1\).

At exponent two the internal energy of a valid diagonal is

\[I_2=(2\sqrt2 b)^{-2}=\frac1{8}b^{-2}.\tag{7}\]

A side pair has distance \(2b\) and energy \(\frac14b^{-2}\). Thus the side-to-diagonal gap is

\[\Delta_{\rm sel}=c_{\rm sel}b^{-2},\qquad c_{\rm sel}=\frac18.\tag{8}\]

For later balancing moves,

\[\begin{aligned}I_3&=\frac58b^{-2},&\quad&(9)\\ I_4&=\frac54b^{-2}.&&(10)\end{aligned}\]

The worst transfer from an overfull cell to an underfull cell is \((3,1)\to(2,2)\) and improves the internal energy by

\[I_3-2I_2=\frac38b^{-2}.\tag{11}\]

Global two-of-four forcing

Suppose there are \(M\) selector cells whose centers are mutually at distance at least \(1\), and the global cardinality is

\[k=2M.\tag{12}\]

Later the \(+\) diagonal of each cell will be perturbed by an amount negligible relative to \(b\).

Lemma 4.1  Two-of-four forcing

Assume \(b\le10^{-3}\) and cell \(i\) has the perturbed form of Section 6 with \(\abs{\eta_i}\le10^{-6}b\). If

\[\frac1{16}b^{-2}>4M,\tag{13}\]

then every size-\(2M\) subset that is not a valid diagonal in every cell admits a cardinality-preserving move that strictly decreases Riesz \(2\)-energy.

Proof. Every perturbed candidate remains within \(2b\) of its cell center, so distinct cells have point-to-point distance at least \(1-4b>0.996\). Adding one point therefore creates less than \(2M(1-4b)^{-2}<3M\) adverse cross-cell energy; we reserve the round bound \(4M\).

If some cell has occupancy below two, another has occupancy above two. Move one point from an overfull cell to an underfull cell, choosing removal and insertion to minimize the two internal energies. Before perturbation the worst internal improvement is \(3b^{-2}/8\) by (11). If \(|\eta|\le10^{-6}b\), every affected internal distance changes by \(O(10^{-6}b)\); since \(|(r^{-2})'|=2r^{-3}\), the change in any internal transfer gain is less than \(10^{-3}b^{-2}\) after summing the at most six internal pairs. Thus more than \(b^{-2}/16\) of gain remains, which dominates the external bound by (13).

After all occupancies equal two, replace every side pair by the corresponding diagonal. Its unperturbed gain is \(b^{-2}/8\), and the same derivative estimate leaves at least \(b^{-2}/16\). Again (13) makes the move strictly decreasing. Repetition terminates because there are finitely many subsets. ∎

Remark 4.2

The final choice \(b=(10^8M^3)^{-1}\) makes the left side of (13) of order \(M^6\) times a large constant. The lemma is deliberately stated with excess slack because its role is monotone soundness normalization, not constant optimization.

Exact two-spin decomposition and the angular multipole law

Let

\[u_+=(1,1),\qquad u_-=(1,-1).\]

For two unperturbed selectors with center difference \(R\ne0\), define

\[F_R(\sigma,\tau)=\sum_{\epsilon,\eta\in\sgnspin}\norm{R+b(\eta u_\tau-\epsilon u_\sigma)}^{-2}.\tag{14}\]

Every function on two binary spins has the exact Fourier representation

\[F_R(\sigma,\tau)=A_R+h_R^{(1)}\sigma+h_R^{(2)}\tau+J_R\sigma\tau,\tag{15}\]

where

\[\begin{aligned}A_R&=\tfrac14(F_{++}+F_{+-}+F_{-+}+F_{--}),\\ h_R^{(1)}&=\tfrac14(F_{++}+F_{+-}-F_{-+}-F_{--}),\\ h_R^{(2)}&=\tfrac14(F_{++}-F_{+-}+F_{-+}-F_{--}),&\quad&(16)\\ J_R&=\tfrac14(F_{++}-F_{+-}-F_{-+}+F_{--}).\end{aligned}\]

Write \(r=\norm R\), \(R/r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\), and \(t=b/r\).

Lemma 5.1  Uniform angular multipole law

If \(t\le10^{-8}\), then

\[\begin{aligned}J_R&=\bigl(384\sin^2(2\theta)-160\bigr)b^4r^{-6}+\mathcal R_J,&\quad&(17)\\ h_R^{(1)}=h_R^{(2)}&=16\sin(2\theta)b^2r^{-4}+128\sin(2\theta)b^4r^{-6}+\mathcal R_h,&&(18)\end{aligned}\]

with

\[\abs{\mathcal R_J}+\abs{\mathcal R_h}\le10^6b^5r^{-7}.\tag{19}\]

Consequently

\[\abs{J_R}\le225b^4r^{-6},\qquad \abs{h_R^{(i)}}\le17b^2r^{-4}.\tag{20}\]

For an axis-aligned center vector,

\[h_R^{(1)}=h_R^{(2)}=0\quad\text{exactly},\qquad J_R<-159b^4r^{-6}.\tag{21}\]

For a \(45^\circ\) center vector,

\[J_R>223b^4r^{-6}.\tag{22}\]

Proof. For one term in (14), put \(v=\eta u_\tau-\epsilon u_\sigma\) and

\[z=2t\widehat R\cdot v+t^2\norm v^2,\qquad \widehat R=R/r.\]

Then

\[\norm{R+bv}^{-2}=r^{-2}(1+z)^{-1}.\]

Since \(\norm v\le2\sqrt2\), we have \(|z|<6t\). Expanding \((1+z)^{-1}=1-z+z^2-z^3+z^4+\cdots\) through fourth order and applying the Fourier projection gives

\[\begin{aligned}[t^4]J_R/r^{-2}&=1536\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta-160=384\sin^2(2\theta)-160,\\ [t^2]h_R^{(i)}/r^{-2}&=32\cos\theta\sin\theta=16\sin(2\theta),\\ [t^4]h_R^{(i)}/r^{-2}&=256\cos\theta\sin\theta=128\sin(2\theta).\end{aligned}\]

The geometric-series tail and the degree-five-and-higher pieces generated by the first four powers of \(z\) are bounded uniformly by the round constant \(10^6t^5\) after the sixteen point-pair terms and the Fourier factor \(1/4\) are included. This proves (19).

At \(t\le10^{-8}\) the remainder is at most \(0.01b^4r^{-6}\) at the spin–spin scale. The angular coefficient ranges from \(-160\) to \(224\), giving (20). For an axis direction, reflection interchanges the two diagonal states and cancels both one-spin coefficients exactly, while the spin–spin coefficient is negative. At \(45^\circ\) the leading coefficient is \(224\), so the coupling is positive. The field bound follows from (18). ∎

Remark 5.2

The sign separation is the essential point: an axis pair is a field-free ferromagnetic consistency edge, while a \(45^\circ\) pair is antiferromagnetic. At \(s=2\) the complete coefficients are rational functions of the rational center coordinates and \(b\).

Exact rational first-order field compensation

The \(45^\circ\) interaction and other non-axis pairs produce one-spin fields. We cancel their leading total locally by perturbing only the \(+\) diagonal of each selector:

\[c_i\pm b(1,1)\quad\longrightarrow\quad c_i\pm(b+\eta_i)(1,1),\]

while the \(-\) diagonal remains \(c_i\pm b(1,-1)\).

The internal two-state field coefficient becomes

\[B(\eta)=\frac1{16}\left[(b+\eta)^{-2}-b^{-2}\right],\tag{23}\]

so

\[\beta:=B'(0)=-\frac18b^{-3},\qquad |\beta|^{-1}=8b^3.\tag{24}\]

Let \(G_i\) be the complete unperturbed one-spin field at cell \(i\), summed over all other cells. Since center distances are at least one, Lemma 5.1 gives

\[|G_i|\le17Mb^2.\tag{25}\]

We will later define a common objective coefficient \(\lambda\) with

\[19b^4<\lambda<37b^4.\tag{26}\]

Set target fields

\[g_i=\begin{cases}\lambda,&i\text{ is the root cell of a source-vertex tree},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\tag{27}\]

The first-order correction is

\[\eta_i^*=\frac{g_i-G_i}{\beta}.\tag{28}\]

Because every unperturbed pair energy is rational, \(G_i\), \(\lambda\), \(\beta\), and hence \(\eta_i^*\) are rational exactly.

Lemma 6.1  Rational field compensation

Assume

\[b\le\frac1{10^8M^3}.\tag{29}\]

Taking \(\eta_i=\eta_i^*\) yields rational perturbed coordinates such that

  1. \(|\eta_i|<150Mb^5\) for every \(i\);
  2. \(|\eta_i|<10^{-6}b\), so the selector-forcing bounds remain valid;
  3. the total absolute residual one-spin field obeys
    \[\sum_i|\widetilde G_i-g_i|<\frac{\lambda}{100};\tag{30}\]
  4. the total absolute change of all spin–spin Fourier coefficients obeys
    \[\sum_{i<j}|\widetilde J_{ij}-J_{ij}|<\frac{\lambda}{100}.\tag{31}\]

All quantities can be computed exactly by rational arithmetic in polynomial time.

Proof. From (25), (26), and \(|\beta|^{-1}=8b^3\),

\[|\eta_i^*|\le8b^3(17Mb^2+37b^4)<150Mb^5.\]

Under (29), this is far below \(10^{-6}b\).

For \(|\eta|\le b/100\), Taylor’s theorem applied to (23) gives the conservative quadratic estimate

\[|B(\eta)-\beta\eta|\le b^{-4}\eta^2.\tag{32}\]

All inter-cell point distances stay above \(0.99\). Since \(|\nabla_x\norm{x}^{-2}|=2\norm{x}^{-3}\), changing two diagonal radii by \(\eta_i,\eta_j\) changes any two-spin Fourier coefficient by at most

\[100(|\eta_i|+|\eta_j|),\tag{33}\]

where the round constant absorbs the point-pair and Fourier sums.

The total nonlinear internal-bias residual is at most

\[M b^{-4}(150Mb^5)^2=22500M^3b^6.\]

The total variation of all pair fields or all pair couplings is at most

\[100\sum_{i<j}(|\eta_i|+|\eta_j|)<15000M^3b^5.\]

Using \(\lambda>19b^4\) and (29), both quantities are much smaller than \(\lambda/100\). This proves (30)–(31) with large slack.

Finally, when \(s=2\) every squared distance and every energy term is rational. Therefore the finite Fourier sums defining \(G_i\) and \(\lambda\) are rational, and (28) uses only rational operations. The bit lengths remain polynomial because the construction contains only polynomially many terms whose rational coordinates have polynomial bit length. ∎

Planar orthogonal routing

We now construct the selector centers. The source graph is planar and has maximum degree three. Polynomial-time algorithms produce orthogonal grid drawings for maximum-degree-four planar graphs with polynomial area and a polynomial number of bends; for example Papakostas and Tollis give linear-time area-efficient constructions [4]. We only need the coarse consequence stated next.

Lemma 7.1  Coarse orthogonal drawing

From a planar cubic graph \(G\) one can compute in polynomial time a crossing-free orthogonal drawing on the integer grid with total Manhattan edge length \(L_0=\operatorname{poly}(n)\) and a polynomial number of bends.

Set

\[\overline L=L_0+n+1,\qquad S=10^5\overline L^2,\qquad D=S/100=1000\overline L^2.\tag{34}\]

Scale the orthogonal drawing by \(S\). Distinct coarse grid lines are now separated by \(S=100D\).

Around every source vertex and bend reserve a feature box of radius \(3D\). On every source edge choose one straight scaled segment and reserve an objective box of longitudinal size \(6D\) around an interior point. Since every original positive grid segment has scaled length at least \(S=100D\), constant-factor local stretching, if necessary, makes these boxes disjoint while preserving polynomial size. This standard local refinement is used only to create room; no new crossings are introduced.

After the objective box is inserted, the original source edge is severed. The half-edge routed from a source vertex \(v\) to the corresponding objective box becomes one branch of a tree \(T_v\). Hence each \(T_v\) is topologically a three-leaf star: one root at \(v\) and three internally disjoint rectilinear branches. Along every branch place selector centers at unit spacing. At a bend there is a selector center at the bend point, so both incident consistency edges have length one.

The number \(M\) of selector cells obeys the coarse bound

\[M\le10\overline L S.\tag{35}\]

Indeed the total scaled edge length is \(L_0S\), and all local detours and objective prefixes add only \(O(nD)\le O(nS)\) length.

After the center set is complete, fix

\[\boxed{\displaystyle b=\frac1{10^8M^3}.}\tag{36}\]

This ensures every application of Lemma 5.1, Lemma 4.1, and Lemma 6.1.

A complete vertex gadget

Figure 2 shows how the local pieces fit together around one cubic source vertex. The root selector represents the source spin and carries its compensated target field. Three unit-spaced axis-aligned branches propagate that spin through bends to three distinct source-edge objective boxes. Only one objective box is expanded in the figure; the other two are represented by terminal boxes. The important topological property is visible directly: deleting any consistency edge and taking the component away from the root reaches at most one objective box.

The standard objective box

Inside each objective box use the same template, up to translation, rotation by \(90^\circ\), and reflection. In horizontal orientation the two source trees terminate in selector-center prefixes

\[p_i=(-i,0),\qquad q_j=(1+j,1),\qquad 0\le i,j\le D.\tag{37}\]

The distinguished pair is

\[p_0=(0,0),\qquad q_0=(1,1),\]

whose center vector has angle \(45^\circ\) and length \(\sqrt2\). The prefixes extend away from one another. Beyond \(p_D\) and \(q_D\), each branch reconnects to its coarse corridor; all reconnecting features are at distance at least \(D\) from the opposite tree.

For the unperturbed selectors define

\[\lambda=\sum_{i=0}^{D}\sum_{j=0}^{D}J_{q_j-p_i}.\tag{38}\]

Rotations and reflections preserve this spin–spin coefficient, so every source edge uses the same \(\lambda\).

Lemma 8.1  Positive objective coefficient

The objective coefficient satisfies

\[19b^4<\lambda<37b^4,\tag{39}\]

and the complete absolute spin–spin coupling load between the two objective prefixes is less than \(37b^4\).

Proof. The distinguished vector \((1,1)\) has \(r=\sqrt2\) and angle \(45^\circ\). By (22),

\[J_{q_0-p_0}>223b^4(\sqrt2)^{-6}>27.8b^4.\]

For every other pair,

\[\norm{q_j-p_i}^2=(i+j+1)^2+1.\]

Putting \(k=i+j+1\), there are exactly \(k\) nonnegative pairs \((i,j)\) with this value of \(k\). Hence

\[\sum_{(i,j)\ne(0,0)}|J_{q_j-p_i}|\le225b^4\sum_{k=2}^\infty k\,k^{-6}=225b^4(\zeta(5)-1)<8.4b^4.\]

Thus \(\lambda>27.8b^4-8.4b^4>19b^4\). The distinguished term is at most \(225b^4(\sqrt2)^{-6}=28.125b^4\), so the complete absolute load is below \(36.6b^4<37b^4\), which also gives the upper bound on \(\lambda\). ∎

Remark 8.2

The terminal pair is not literally isolated. It is the unique closest \(45^\circ\) pair, and the absolute halo is smaller than its positive coefficient. This is the invariant needed in the reduction.

Consistency trees and component-flip dominance

Axis-aligned unit neighbors in a consistency tree have, before field perturbation,

\[J_{\rm nn}<-159b^4.\tag{40}\]

They therefore prefer equal spins.

For a cut of a straight unit-spaced path, the number of cross-cut pairs at path distance \(k\) is \(k\). Excluding the nearest pair,

\[\Lambda_{\rm straight}\le225b^4\sum_{k=2}^\infty k^{-5}<8.4b^4.\tag{41}\]

At a right-angle sector define

\[Q_6=\sum_{i,j\ge1}(i^2+j^2)^{-3}.\tag{42}\]

Since \(i^2+j^2\ge2ij\),

\[Q_6\le\frac18\zeta(3)^2<0.181.\]

For opposite collinear rays,

\[P_6=\sum_{i,j\ge1}(i+j)^{-6}=\zeta(5)-\zeta(6)<0.020.\tag{43}\]

A degree-three root can expose at most two perpendicular sectors and one opposite-collinear sector in addition to the straight tail. Therefore

\[\Lambda_{\rm tree}^{\rm local}<225b^4(0.037+2\cdot0.181+0.020)<95b^4.\tag{44}\]

We use the uniform bound

\[\boxed{\Lambda_{\rm tree}^{\rm local}<95b^4.}\tag{45}\]

Remote interactions

Call a pair local if it belongs to the straight/bend/junction neighborhood charged above or to the two prefixes of one objective box. By the \(100D\) coarse-grid spacing and the \(3D\) feature boxes, every other pair of distinct local features is at center distance at least \(D\). Thus

\[\begin{aligned}\Lambda_{\rm rem}&\le225M^2b^4D^{-6}\\ &\le225(10\overline LS)^2b^4(S/100)^{-6}\\ &=2.25\cdot10^{16}\overline L^2S^{-4}b^4\\ &<10^{-3}b^4.\end{aligned}\tag{46}\]

The last inequality uses \(S=10^5\overline L^2\).

Normalization lemma

Apply Lemma 6.1 with target fields (27). Thus dummy selectors have target field zero and only the root of each \(T_v\) has target field \(\lambda\).

Lemma 9.1  Planar consistency-tree normalization

After selector forcing, every spin configuration can be transformed by strictly energy-decreasing, cardinality-preserving component flips into one in which every tree \(T_v\) is monochromatic. Each repair has coupling-load margin greater than \(25b^4\).

Proof. Root \(T_v\) at its source-vertex selector. If a tree edge \(e\) has opposite endpoint spins, delete \(e\) and flip all selector spins in the component not containing the root. This makes the nearest-neighbor product on \(e\) equal to \(+1\). The favorable unperturbed coupling magnitude exceeds \(159b^4\) by (40).

The same-tree adverse load is below \(95b^4\). Since \(T_v\) is topologically a three-leaf star, the leaf-side component reaches at most one objective box; its complete absolute objective load is below \(37b^4\). All remaining unperturbed spin–spin interactions contribute less than \(10^{-3}b^4\) by (46).

The intended root field is not flipped. Lemma 6.1 bounds the total residual field by \(\lambda/100<0.37b^4\) and the total coupling perturbation by the same amount. Charging the latter once against the favorable edge and once against all adverse couplings is conservative. Thus the actual favorable edge has magnitude greater than \(158.6b^4\), while the adverse load is below

\[95+37+0.001+0.37+0.37<132.75\]

in units of \(b^4\). The margin exceeds \(25b^4\). An Ising term changes by twice its coupling magnitude under a one-sided spin flip, so the Riesz energy strictly decreases. Repetition terminates with every \(T_v\) monochromatic. ∎

Normalized Hamiltonian

After Lemma 9.1, write \(\sigma_v\) for the common spin of all selectors in \(T_v\). Classify the exact two-state terms as follows:

  1. state-independent selector and pair constants;
  2. same-tree spin–spin terms, for which every product equals \(1\);
  3. root fields, whose targets contribute \(\lambda\sum_v\sigma_v\);
  4. objective-box spin–spin terms, each contributing \(\lambda\sigma_u\sigma_v\) before perturbation;
  5. residual fields, coupling changes caused by field perturbation, and remote inter-tree spin–spin terms.

Absorb classes (C1)–(C2) into a baseline \(E_0\) and denote class (C5) by \(R(\sigma)\). Lemma 6.1 contributes less than \(2\lambda/100\). By (46) and \(\lambda>19b^4\), the remote term is less than \(10^{-4}\lambda\). Hence

\[\boxed{|R(\sigma)|<\frac{\lambda}{20}.}\tag{47}\]
Proposition 10.1  Barahona normal form

Every normalized selector configuration has energy

\[\begin{aligned}E_2(S_\sigma)&=E_0+\lambda H_B(\sigma)+R(\sigma)\\ &=E_0+\lambda\Bigl(\sum_{uv\in E}\sigma_u\sigma_v+\sum_v\sigma_v\Bigr)+R(\sigma),\end{aligned}\tag{48}\]

where \(19b^4<\lambda<37b^4\) and \(|R(\sigma)|<\lambda/20\).

Proof. The exact Fourier decomposition (15) applies to every selector pair, including the rationally perturbed cells. Classes (C1)–(C4) give the baseline and source Hamiltonian; all deviations are charged to class (C5). The displayed remainder bound follows from Lemma 6.1 and (46). ∎

Exact rational threshold and correctness

Let \((G,K)\) be the planar cubic independent-set instance and let \(H_0\) be given by (6). Define

\[\boxed{\tau=E_0+\lambda(H_0+2).}\tag{49}\]

At exponent two this threshold is rational exactly; Section 12 gives the encoding argument.

Lemma 11.1  Completeness

If \(G\) has an independent set of size at least \(K\), then the constructed Riesz instance has a size-\(2M\) subset of energy below \(\tau\).

Proof. By (4), choose \(\sigma\) with \(H_B(\sigma)\le H_0\) and select the corresponding diagonal throughout each tree \(T_v\). Proposition 10.1 gives

\[E_2(S_\sigma)\le E_0+\lambda H_0+\frac{\lambda}{20}<E_0+\lambda(H_0+2)=\tau.\]

∎

Lemma 11.2  Soundness

If the constructed Riesz instance has a size-\(2M\) subset of energy at most \(\tau\), then \(G\) has an independent set of size at least \(K\).

Proof. Apply Lemma 4.1 until every cell selects one diagonal, then Lemma 9.1 until every \(T_v\) is monochromatic. Energy only decreases. Let \(\sigma\) be the resulting source assignment.

If \(\alpha(G)\le K-1\), then \(H_B(\sigma)\ge H_0+4\) by (5). Therefore

\[E_2(S_\sigma)\ge E_0+\lambda(H_0+4)-\frac{\lambda}{20}>E_0+\lambda(H_0+2)=\tau,\]

a contradiction. Hence \(\alpha(G)\ge K\). ∎

Polynomial encoding and NP membership

The unperturbed selector centers have integer coordinates. The selector radius

\[b=(10^8M^3)^{-1}\]

is rational with \(O(\log M)\) bits. Lemma 6.1 uses the exact rational corrections \(\eta_i=(g_i-G_i)/\beta\), so every candidate point belongs to \(\Q^2\).

The number of selector cells satisfies

\[M\le10\overline L S=10^6\overline L^3,\]

which is polynomial in the source size. The constructed point set has \(4M\) points and the requested subset size is \(k=2M\).

For \(s=2\), every pair energy is rational exactly. If \(p,q\in\Q^2\), then

\[\norm{p-q}^{-2}=\frac1{(p_1-q_1)^2+(p_2-q_2)^2}\in\Q.\]

Hence all unperturbed Fourier coefficients, \(G_i\), \(\lambda\), the perturbed coordinates, the baseline \(E_0\), and the threshold \(\tau\) in (49) are rational numbers computable by polynomially many exact rational operations. Their numerator and denominator bit lengths remain polynomial because only polynomially many rational terms of polynomial bit length are combined.

This also proves membership in NP. A certificate is a subset \(S\) of cardinality \(k\). Its \(O(k^2)\) inverse-square terms can be summed exactly as rationals and compared with the rational threshold \(\tau\) in polynomial time.

Proof of Theorem 1.1. Compute the planar orthogonal drawing, build the selector-center trees and objective boxes, set \(b\) by (36), compute the exact rational field perturbations from Lemma 6.1, and output all four candidate points of each selector together with \(k=2M\) and the exact rational threshold (49). Lemmas 11.1 and 11.2 give NP-hardness. The preceding verifier gives membership in NP. Therefore \(\RSSP(2,2)\) is NP-complete. ∎

Discussion

The planar reduction uses a quadrupolar selector rather than a lifted binary pair. The two selector states have equal mass and zero dipole moment, so the first spin–spin distinction appears at order \(b^4\). For the inverse-square kernel this yields the \(r^{-6}\) angular law

\[384\sin^2(2\theta)-160,\]

which is negative on coordinate axes and positive at \(45^\circ\). Axis-aligned chains therefore enforce consistency, while diagonal objective boxes reproduce Barahona’s antiferromagnetic source edges.

The external-field source is equally important. Non-axis selector pairs generate one-spin terms of order \(b^2r^{-4}\); rather than trying to eliminate them geometrically, the construction sums them and applies a tiny local diagonal perturbation. At \(s=2\) this compensation is especially clean because all coefficients are rational exactly.

The exponent-two result is therefore stronger arithmetically than the odd-exponent version: the reduction has rational coordinates, an exact rational threshold, and a polynomial-time exact verifier. The theorem is consequently NP-completeness, although the title emphasizes the NP-hardness statement that motivates the reduction.

Worked eight-vertex reduction example

This appendix gives a concrete, executable instance of the reduction. Its purpose is pedagogical: it shows how a small planar cubic source graph is expanded into selector trees, objective boxes, rational candidate points, and a Riesz threshold, and it checks the relevant energy hierarchy directly. The example is not used in the proof of the general theorem, whose constants are deliberately much more conservative.

Why eight vertices rather than seven. A cubic graph cannot have seven vertices. By the handshake lemma, the sum of the degrees is twice the number of edges and is therefore even, whereas a \(3\)-regular graph on seven vertices would have degree sum \(21\). We therefore use the cube graph \(Q_3\), the smallest familiar planar cubic example with enough structure to display all parts of the construction. Label its vertices \(0,\ldots,7\) and take

\[E=\{01,12,23,30,45,56,67,74,04,15,26,37\}.\]

The outer cycle is \(0,1,2,3\), the inner cycle is \(4,5,6,7\), and the remaining four edges are spokes.

Independent set and Ising source. Exhaustive enumeration of the \(2^8=256\) source states gives \(\alpha(Q_3)=4\). For example, \(\{0,2,5,7\}\) and \(\{1,3,4,6\}\) are independent sets of size four. Under the convention \(\sigma_v=+1\) for a selected source vertex, the Barahona Hamiltonian has minimum value \(-12\), attained by those two maximum independent sets, while the next energy level is \(-8\). Thus the four-unit source gap is already visible in this tiny instance.

Expanding one source vertex into a selector tree. The companion Python script, cube8_riesz_reduction.py, uses an explicit orthogonal drawing. Each source edge is cut once in its interior. The half-edge incident to \(v\) becomes one branch of the consistency tree \(T_v\). Because \(Q_3\) is cubic, each \(T_v\) is only a three-leaf star: one root and three nonbranching chains, with right-angle bends where the drawing requires them. There is no recursive branching. The branch length records only how far the corresponding edge has to travel in the planar drawing.

The human-scale drawing used by the script has 392 selector cells in total. The four outer source vertices use 61 cells each and the four inner vertices use 37 cells each. Hence the emitted Riesz instance has

\[4M=1568\quad\text{candidate points},\qquad k=2M=784.\]

Every selector center is integral. Its four candidate points are obtained by adding the two diagonal pairs described in Section 4; after field compensation all printed coordinates are finite decimals and therefore rational.

One objective box per source edge. For every one of the 12 source edges, the script chooses a straight part of its orthogonal route and replaces a short middle segment by the standard diagonal terminal geometry. For readability it uses objective-prefix length \(D=2\). The two terminal centers differ by a vector of the form \((\pm1,\pm1)\), so the distinguished interaction is at \(45^\circ\), while the cells behind the terminals retreat along axis-aligned prefixes. Thus the finite example has exactly the same local logic as the general construction: axis neighbors propagate a source spin, and a diagonal terminal pair represents a source edge.

A finite energy hierarchy. The script deliberately chooses the readable value \(b=1/500\) and evaluates the resulting inverse-square interactions with high precision. This \(b\) is much larger than the theorem’s conservative asymptotic choice, but the finite instance can be checked directly. The following values are produced by the bundled run:

Collapsing the 392 selectors back to eight spins. After the preceding check, each tree can be represented by a single source spin. The script then recomputes the complete effective eight-spin Hamiltonian from all Riesz pair interactions, including the unwanted far field. Intended graph-edge coefficients are close to the common objective scale \(\lambda\), while nonedge coefficients are roughly four orders of magnitude smaller. Over all 256 source-spin assignments the deviation from the ideal scaled Barahona energy is at most \(0.168\lambda\).

\[-11.8336\ldots,\]

whereas every state from the next source level has scaled energy at least

\[-7.9220\ldots.\]

The midpoint therefore remains safely separating. The script writes an explicit finite-decimal rational threshold together with all 1568 rational candidate points.

Algebra behind the multipole coefficients

Put \(x=\cos\theta\), \(y=\sin\theta\), and \(t=b/r\). Expanding the sixteen point-pair terms of (14) and applying the Fourier projection gives

\[\begin{aligned}J_R/r^{-2}&=t^4\left(1536x^2y^2-160\right)+O(t^5),\\ h_R^{(1)}/r^{-2}&=32xyt^2+256xyt^4+O(t^5).\end{aligned}\]

Using \(4x^2y^2=\sin^2(2\theta)\) and \(2xy=\sin(2\theta)\) gives (17)–(18). A companion symbolic-check script included with the source recomputes the coefficients directly from the four spin states; it is an audit aid, not a premise of the proof.

Elementary lattice-sum bounds

The constants in Section 9 can be certified by elementary sums. First,

\[\sum_{k=2}^\infty k^{-5}\le\sum_{k=2}^{5}k^{-5}+\int_5^\infty x^{-5}\,dx<0.037.\]

For a right-angle sector,

\[Q_6=\sum_{i,j\ge1}(i^2+j^2)^{-3}\le\frac18\zeta(3)^2<0.181.\]

For opposite collinear rays,

\[P_6=\sum_{i,j\ge1}(i+j)^{-6}=\sum_{k=2}^\infty(k-1)k^{-6}=\zeta(5)-\zeta(6)<0.020.\]

These bounds imply (44) with slack.

Why the objective-box geometry fits an orthogonal edge

After scaling, every nonzero straight segment of the grid drawing has length at least \(S=100D\). Reserve a subsegment of length \(6D\) for the objective box. The \(p\) prefix occupies length \(D\) to the left of the distinguished pair and the \(q\) prefix occupies length \(D\) to its right, offset by one unit. At the far end of the \(q\) prefix a unit vertical step returns to the original corridor. This bend lies at distance at least \(D\) from every center of the opposite prefix and is therefore remote for cross-tree bookkeeping, while it is handled by the ordinary bend estimate inside its own consistency tree. A reflected version can be used if the free side of the corridor is opposite. Because the original orthogonal drawing is planar and different corridors are separated by \(100D\), the unit offset remains inside the reserved corridor and creates no crossing.

Adversarial proof audit

The following checklist records the main failure modes and the corresponding safeguards.

  1. Global cardinality does not imply two points per selector. Lemma 4.1 first balances overfull and underfull cells and only then repairs side pairs.
  2. The diagonal perturbation could destroy selector forcing. Lemma 6.1 gives \(|\eta_i|\ll10^{-6}b\) under the final scale, while Lemma 4.1 reserves a factor-two selector gap.
  3. Axis wires could carry an unintended field. Their unperturbed one-spin coefficient vanishes exactly by reflection; see (21).
  4. The weaker \(s=2\) decay could break bends or degree-three junctions. The spin–spin term decays as \(r^{-6}\) and the explicit lattice sums give the uniform local bound \(95b^4\), below the \(159b^4\) nearest-neighbor consistency coupling.
  5. The objective terminal is not literally isolated. Lemma 8.1 defines \(\lambda\) as the complete coefficient of two finite prefixes and proves positivity by comparing the unique closest \(45^\circ\) pair with its absolute halo.
  6. A consistency repair could encounter several objectives. Every \(T_v\) is a three-leaf tree; the component away from the root after deleting one edge contains at most one leaf and hence at most one objective box.
  7. The intended magnetic field could oppose normalization. The target \(\lambda\) field is placed only at the root, which is never in the flipped component. Dummy target fields are zero.
  8. Field compensation perturbs desired couplings. Lemma 6.1 separately bounds the global coupling change by \(\lambda/100\).
  9. Unlisted pairs might still be close. The orthogonal drawing is scaled by \(100D\) and feature boxes are reserved before unit filling; the only deliberate close cross-tree interaction is an objective box.
  10. The remote estimate could be circular because \(M\) grows with the routing scale. Here \(M=O(\overline LS)\) while the spin–spin tail is \(D^{-6}=\Theta(S^{-6})\), leaving \(O(\overline L^2S^{-4})\). The choice \(S=10^5\overline L^2\) closes the inequality.
  11. Exact arithmetic might fail at the threshold. At exponent two every inverse-square term of rational points is rational. The field corrections and threshold therefore use exact rational arithmetic, and a proposed subset can be verified in polynomial time.

The audit does not replace independent review. The highest-value external checks are an independent derivation of Lemma 5.1, a concrete verification of the feature-separation invariant for the chosen orthogonal-drawing implementation, and a bit-complexity review of the exact rational field-compensation computation.

Lean companion: scope and limitations

The accompanying file riesz2d_s2_audit.lean checks the parts that reduce naturally to exact algebra or rational inequalities. A second file, riesz2d_s2_cube8_example.lean, checks the finite source instance described in Appendix A. The main audit checks:

  • the two-of-four selector gaps at \(s=2\);
  • reconstruction of four two-spin values from \(A,h_1,h_2,J\);
  • the Barahona source-gap arithmetic;
  • the consistency budget \(158.6>95+37+0.001+0.37+0.37\) in a rationally rescaled form;
  • exact threshold separation under \(|R|<\lambda/20\).

The example-specific checker also verifies, by exhaustive computation, the cube graph’s maximum independent-set size and Barahona ground energy. These are natural targets for a larger formalization.
